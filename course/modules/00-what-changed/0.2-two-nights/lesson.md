title: Two nights, same sky
question: Our two images show the same sky on two nights. What is different between them, and how can you tell?
terms: [observing conditions, real change, pointing, seeing, PSF]
---

Two pictures of the same sky can differ for two kinds of reason. Something in
the sky really changed: a [[real change]]. Or the [[observing conditions]]
changed: where exactly the telescope was aimed (its [[pointing]]), how bright
the background was, the exposure time, and the [[seeing]]: the turbulence in
the air that blurs each star into a small disc, more on some nights than others.

For this pair the exposure time is the same on both nights. We call night 1
the [[reference image]] and night 2 the [[target image]].

:::closure
found: Two pictures of the same sky differ for two kinds of reason. A few differences are real changes in the sky. Most come from the observing conditions: pointing, background, seeing. You told them apart with evidence, a star that moved, stars that grew wider, a floor that rose, not by assuming.
matters: This is the central difficulty of the whole field. A real change, say a faint new supernova, is usually far smaller than the differences the night itself makes. Every step of the pipeline in this course exists to remove one kind of observing-condition difference, so that what survives can be trusted.
raises: Can we simply subtract the reference from the target and see only the real changes?
:::
